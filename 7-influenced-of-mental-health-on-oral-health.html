Seven Ways Mental Health Can Affect Your Teeth

30 March 2022·7 min read

Oral health is not separate from the rest of life. When someone is under sustained stress, struggling with their mood, or managing a mental health condition, it usually shows up in their mouth eventually. Understanding how that happens makes it easier to protect your teeth through a difficult period, and easier to be kind to yourself about it afterwards.

It Works in Both Directions

The connection runs both ways. Research has linked stress, low mood, anxiety and loneliness with gum disease, and sustained stress raises cortisol, which is thought to affect how the gums respond to bacteria. People living with severe mental illness are more likely to develop gum disease. In the other direction, problems such as missing or misaligned teeth, difficulty with speech and persistent bad breath can knock confidence and make social situations harder, and social connection is itself important for mental health.

1. Grinding and Clenching

Stress is one of the strongest associations with bruxism. Clenching during the day and grinding at night place heavy forces on teeth, jaw joints and muscles, and over time they wear enamel flat, crack teeth and loosen restorations.

Common signs are jaw ache or headache on waking, teeth that look shorter than they used to, and increasing sensitivity. A custom splint for grinding and clenching protects the teeth while you work on the cause.

2. Routines That Slip

When energy is low, the two-minute brush and the daily floss are among the first things to go. Depression in particular makes routine self-care genuinely difficult, and weeks of missed cleaning are enough for gum inflammation and new decay to begin.

If that is where you are, lower the bar rather than abandoning it. Brushing once, imperfectly, is far better than not at all. Keep a brush somewhere visible. Use a brush with a timer if decision-making feels hard. Fluoride mouth rinse on a bad day adds some protection.

3. Dry Mouth From Medication

Many medications used for anxiety, depression and other conditions reduce saliva. Saliva neutralises acid, washes away debris and carries minerals that repair early enamel damage, so less of it means a meaningfully higher decay risk, often on surfaces that were never a problem before.

Anxiety and stress can reduce saliva on their own, too, and a persistently dry mouth also raises the risk of mouth ulcers and infection. Do not stop a prescribed medication over this. Instead, tell us you take it, and mention the dryness to your prescribing doctor, who may be able to review the medication. We can recommend high-fluoride toothpaste, saliva substitutes and more frequent monitoring, and simple habits such as sipping water and chewing sugar-free gum help.

4. Changes in Diet and Drinking

Low mood and stress often shift what and how we eat. Some people lose their appetite and miss meals, which can leave the diet short of nutrients such as calcium that teeth and gums rely on. Grazing on sugary food, energy drinks for a flagging afternoon, more coffee, more alcohol, and comfort eating late at night all increase the number of acid attacks your teeth face each day.

Frequency matters more than total quantity, so where you can, keep sweet things to mealtimes rather than spread across the day, and drink water in between.

5. Smoking, Vaping and Other Substances

Smoking and vaping often increase during stressful periods. Both affect gum health, and smoking masks the bleeding that would otherwise warn you gum disease is developing, so the condition can progress further before anyone notices. Some recreational substances also cause severe dry mouth, heavy grinding and rapid decay.

We are not here to lecture. Telling us honestly simply means we monitor the right things.

6. Avoiding the Dentist

Dental anxiety and general anxiety feed each other, and avoidance is a completely understandable response. The difficulty is that problems grow quietly, and the eventual visit involves more treatment, which reinforces the fear.

There is a way back. Our approach with anxious patients starts with a conversation rather than an examination, moves at your pace and can break treatment into small, manageable steps. Nobody will comment on how long it has been.

7. Physical Effects of Disordered Eating

Conditions involving frequent vomiting expose teeth to stomach acid, which erodes enamel, typically on the inner surfaces of the upper front teeth. Teeth become sensitive, thin and translucent at the edges.

We raise this gently and confidentially when we see the pattern, because the dental damage is treatable and, more importantly, because support exists for the underlying condition. If this is something you are managing, tell us only if you want to, and we will focus on protecting your teeth either way. Rinsing with water or a fluoride mouth rinse rather than brushing immediately afterwards helps reduce erosion.

Practical Protection During a Hard Period

  • Aim for one thorough brush a day if two is not achievable
  • Use fluoride toothpaste and spit without rinsing
  • Keep water nearby and sip it rather than sweet drinks
  • Chew sugar-free gum when brushing is not possible
  • Keep your check-up and clean appointments, even if nothing hurts
  • Mention new medications so we can adjust your preventive care for adults
  • Wear your splint if you have one
  • Drink fluoridated tap water where you can
  • Keep up regular meals and some physical activity, which supports both mood and oral health

Catching Up Later

If a period of poor oral care has left its mark, the fix is usually less daunting than people expect. A thorough clean, gum and periodontal treatment if the gums need it, and a few white fillings will often bring things back on track, and we can stage the work so it is manageable.

If you are struggling with your mental health, please speak with your GP or another health professional. Support is available, and looking after your teeth is easier when the rest is being looked after too.

How We Try to Make It Easier

A dental practice can either add to the pressure or take some off. We aim for the second.

That means appointments where you are told what is happening before it happens, treatment broken into stages rather than marathon sessions, and honest, calm explanations without alarm. It also means not moralising. If you tell us you have been brushing once a week for six months, the useful response is a plan, not a lecture.

Practical things help too: a written quote so cost is not an unknown, the option of a consultation with no treatment, and the freedom to stop at any point. If you need to reschedule on a bad day, that is fine. We would rather you came later than not at all.
