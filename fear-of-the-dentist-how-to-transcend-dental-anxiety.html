Overcoming a Fear of the Dentist

5 October 2023·7 min read

Dental anxiety is far more common than most people assume, and it exists on a wide spectrum. For some it is mild unease in the waiting room. For others it means years of avoidance, a racing heart at the thought of booking, and a growing sense of dread about what might be found.

If that is you, the most useful thing to know is that the situation is workable. Not by being braver, but by changing how the appointment is structured around you.

Where the Fear Usually Comes From

Understanding the source helps, because different fears call for different responses.

  • A past experience. A painful treatment, particularly in childhood, teaches the nervous system to expect the same again.
  • Loss of control. Lying back, unable to speak or see what is happening, is uncomfortable for many people regardless of what is being done.
  • Needles. A specific, very common fear that has little to do with dentistry itself.
  • Sounds and smells. The high-pitched drill and the distinctive clinical smell can trigger a response before anything begins.
  • Embarrassment. Worry about being judged for the condition of your teeth, or for how long it has been.
  • Gagging. A sensitive gag reflex makes even routine procedures feel threatening.
  • Cost. Uncertainty about what treatment will cost adds a layer of anxiety of its own.

Avoidance is a rational response to fear in the short term, but it tends to cost more later. Small problems become larger ones, and the eventual visit involves more treatment, which reinforces the fear. Untreated infection and gum disease can also affect your general health, not just your teeth. Breaking that loop early is the aim.

Anxiety, Fear or Phobia?

These words are often used interchangeably, but they describe different intensities. Dental anxiety is unease about the unknown: what will be found, what will be done. Fear is a stronger reaction to something specific, such as needles or the drill. Dental phobia is intense and overwhelming, often leading to complete avoidance. Anxiety and fear usually ease with the right approach at the practice. A phobia may benefit from working with a psychologist alongside your dental team, and there is no shame in that.

What Genuinely Helps

Say So When You Book

Tell our team you feel anxious when you make the appointment. It changes practical things: how much time is allowed, how the visit is structured, and how we talk you through it.

Start Without Treatment

A first appointment can be a conversation. No instruments, no examination unless you want one. You see the practice, meet the person who would be treating you, and describe what worries you most. Many people find this single step lowers the barrier considerably.

Agree on a Stop Signal

A raised hand that means stop, honoured every time, restores a sense of control. Knowing the treatment will pause on request is often more reassuring than the pause itself.

Ask for Explanations, or Not

Some people are calmer knowing exactly what is coming. Others prefer not to hear the details. Tell us which you are, and we will match it.

Use Distraction

Noise-cancelling headphones, your own music or podcast, and a ceiling-mounted television all help break the association between the sounds of a practice and the feeling of dread. Slow, controlled breathing out for longer than you breathe in genuinely settles the nervous system.

Know How Pain Is Controlled

Modern dentistry is considerably more comfortable than many people remember. Local anaesthetic remains the foundation of pain control: a numbing gel is applied to the gum first, then the area is anaesthetised so the nerves stop sending pain signals. We check that the tooth is fully numb before starting, and you can ask for a top-up at any point.

Prepare Your Mind Beforehand

A few quiet minutes before the appointment, using slow breathing, meditation or simply sitting calmly, makes it easier to stay settled in the chair. Some people find it helps to picture the appointment going smoothly and to remind themselves why they are going.

Consider Happy Gas

Happy gas, or nitrous oxide, is breathed through a small nose mask. It helps many people feel calm and detached from the procedure while staying awake and able to respond, and its effects wear off quickly afterwards. The amount is adjusted to suit you. Some people do not remember every detail of the visit afterwards, and a small number dislike the sensation, in which case other options can be discussed. For some patients, a prescribed oral sedative taken beforehand may be discussed after a review of medical history, in which case you will need someone to drive you home.

Break Treatment Into Stages

There is rarely a need to do everything at once. Prioritising an urgent toothache first, then tackling the rest in manageable appointments, is usually far easier than one long visit.

Book at the Right Time

Morning appointments mean less time spent anticipating. Avoid scheduling straight after a stressful commitment.

What About Children?

Fear is often learned, so it is worth being careful with language around children. Avoid words like needle, drill and pain, even reassuringly, and let them form their own impressions. Early, uneventful children's dentistry visits, where nothing is done beyond counting teeth and a ride in the chair, build familiarity before any treatment is needed.

A Note on Judgement

People who have avoided the dentist for years often expect a lecture. It does not happen here. We are interested in where you are now and what you would like to achieve, not in what happened in between. If your gums need attention, we will explain gum and periodontal treatment and plan it in stages. If you need a check-up and clean, we will do that at a pace you can manage.

What a First Appointment Can Look Like

For someone who has not attended in years, the first visit is usually far less confronting than they imagined. A typical version goes something like this.

You arrive and let our team know you are nervous. You sit in the chair upright, not reclined, and talk through what has kept you away and what you want to avoid. If you are comfortable, we look in your mouth with a mirror and a camera, with nothing sharp involved. We might take X-rays, or leave that for another day.

Then we explain what we have seen, in plain terms and without dramatics, and outline what could be done in what order. You leave with a plan, and you decide what happens next and when.

No treatment is carried out at that visit unless you want it to be. For many anxious patients, simply getting through the door and finding that nothing bad happened is the turning point.
