Why Sour Lollies Are Especially Hard on Teeth

5 October 2023·6 min read

All lollies are a decay risk, but sour varieties are a particular problem, and the reason is worth understanding because it applies to a lot of other things too.

Three Problems at Once

High Sugar

Bacteria in plaque metabolise the sugar and produce acid, which dissolves minerals out of enamel. That is the familiar mechanism behind tooth decay.

Direct Acid

Sour lollies get their flavour from added acids, commonly citric, malic or tartaric acid. These attack enamel directly, without needing bacteria at all. Many sour products have a pH comparable to or lower than soft drinks and, in some cases, approaching that of lemon juice.

This is erosion rather than decay, and it affects whole surfaces rather than creating a localised cavity. Teeth become thinner, more sensitive, more translucent at the edges and more yellow, because thinner enamel reveals more of the dentine beneath.

Long Contact Time

A sour lolly is sucked or chewed slowly, often one after another from the packet. The combination of sugar and acid is held against the teeth for an extended period, and sticky varieties lodge in the grooves of back teeth and between teeth, where they remain long after the packet is finished.

Effects on Soft Tissue

The acidity can also affect the tongue, cheeks and gums. Consumer testing of sour lollies sold in Australia has found them well below the pH at which enamel begins to dissolve, and there have been reports of children developing sore, burnt or ulcerated tongues after holding very sour lollies in their mouths for long periods.

Why It Matters More for Children and Teenagers

Newly erupted enamel is less mineralised and more vulnerable for the first couple of years after a tooth appears, which coincides with the ages at which sour lollies are most popular. Deep grooves in new back teeth also trap sticky residue.

Teenagers face the same issue alongside energy drinks and soft drinks, which is why preventive care for young adults focuses so heavily on acid and frequency.

The Damage Looks Different From Ordinary Decay

Erosion from acidic foods tends to show as:

  • A smooth, shiny appearance on the biting surfaces
  • Edges of front teeth becoming thin and see-through
  • Cupping or shallow dents on the chewing surfaces of back teeth
  • Increasing sensitivity to cold and sweetness
  • Teeth looking more yellow as enamel thins
  • Fillings appearing to stand slightly proud of the tooth surface, because the enamel around them has worn away

Enamel does not regenerate, so this damage is permanent. The aim is to stop it progressing.

Practical Harm Reduction

Telling people never to eat lollies does not work. These do:

  • Keep them to one sitting, ideally after a meal, rather than grazing through a packet across an afternoon
  • Rinse with water straight afterwards to clear sugar and dilute acid
  • Do not brush for about 30 minutes, because enamel is temporarily softened and brushing immediately wears it away
  • Chew sugar-free gum afterwards to stimulate saliva, which buffers acid and helps repair early damage
  • Choose chocolate over sour and sticky options where there is a choice, as it clears the mouth faster and is less acidic
  • Have water, milk or cheese afterwards, which help neutralise acid
  • Avoid them before bed, since saliva flow drops overnight and the acid sits undisturbed

Rinsing, Timing and Saliva

Three small habits do most of the work in limiting damage from anything sweet or sour.

Rinsing with plain water immediately afterwards dilutes acid and clears residual sugar, which shortens the acid attack considerably. It takes seconds and requires nothing you do not already have.

Timing matters because saliva is your natural defence. Eating sour lollies with or just after a meal means saliva flow is already high. Eating them alone in the afternoon, or in bed, means the opposite.

Chewing sugar-free gum for ten minutes afterwards raises saliva flow substantially and speeds up the return to a neutral pH.

For Parents

Lollies are part of childhood, and a confrontational approach usually backfires. Setting treat times rather than leaving packets accessible, offering water alongside, and making it part of a meal all reduce the damage without turning it into a battle. Our children's dentistry visits include this kind of practical advice, without lecturing.

Fissure sealants in the deep grooves of new back teeth are also worth discussing, as they protect exactly the surfaces that sticky lollies target.

If Damage Has Already Occurred

Early erosion and white spot decay can sometimes be stabilised with fluoride, reduced frequency and better home care. Established cavities need white fillings, and significant erosion may eventually require more extensive restoration.

Sensitivity from thinned enamel often responds to a desensitising toothpaste. Worth knowing: teeth whitening works best on healthy enamel, and eroded, sensitive teeth may need treating and settling first.

What About Other Sour Foods?

The same principles apply well beyond the lolly aisle, and the usual suspects are easy to overlook.

Citrus fruit and juice, vinegar-based dressings, kombucha, sparkling water with added flavouring, sports drinks, wine and many flavoured sparkling drinks are all acidic enough to erode enamel over time.

None of them needs to be avoided. What matters is contact time and frequency: drink them with a meal rather than across an afternoon, use a straw for cold drinks where practical, rinse with water afterwards, and leave brushing for half an hour.
