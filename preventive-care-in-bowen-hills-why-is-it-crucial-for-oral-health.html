What Preventive Dental Care Actually Involves

29 June 2019·5 min read

Prevention is one of those words that sounds worthy and vague. In dentistry it has a specific meaning: identifying what puts your teeth at risk, reducing those risks, and intervening early enough that treatment stays small.

It is also the part of dentistry with the best return. The cheapest, most comfortable version of any dental problem is the one caught before it causes symptoms.

Risk Is Personal

Two people can brush identically and have very different outcomes, because risk is made up of many factors:

  • Saliva flow, which is reduced by many common medications
  • Diet, and particularly how often you eat and drink through the day
  • Existing restorations, since every margin is a potential site for new decay
  • Gum condition and history of periodontal disease
  • Gum recession exposing softer root surfaces
  • Grinding habits
  • Smoking
  • Medical conditions such as diabetes
  • Previous decay, which remains the strongest predictor of future decay

A useful prevention plan starts by working out which of these apply to you, rather than giving everyone the same advice.

What a Prevention Plan Includes

Regular Examination

Examinations find early decay, wear, cracks, gum changes and soft tissue concerns while they are still simple to address. X-rays are taken at intervals suited to your risk, because much of what matters happens between teeth and below the gum.

Professional Cleaning

Tartar cannot be removed at home, and the areas beneath it keep causing inflammation. A check-up and clean deals with it and resets the baseline.

Fluoride

Fluoride strengthens enamel and supports repair of early damage. Beyond toothpaste, this may include professional applications at your visit, high-fluoride toothpaste for higher-risk patients, or a fluoride mouth rinse.

Fissure Sealants

Thin coatings placed in the deep grooves of back teeth, where a brush cannot reach the base. Particularly valuable in children's dentistry, and sometimes for adults with deep grooves.

Protective Appliances

A night splint protects teeth from grinding and clenching, and custom mouthguards protect against sporting injuries. Both prevent damage that no amount of cleaning can.

Tailored Home Care

Not generic advice, but the specific technique, brush type and interdental size that suit your mouth, plus dietary suggestions aimed at frequency rather than restriction.

Monitoring Over Time

Records, photographs and measurements allow us to compare year to year. A white spot that has not changed in three years is a different situation from one that has grown, and only a record can tell you which you have.

Prevention at Different Stages of Life

Children benefit most from early familiarity, sealants, fluoride and dietary habits that stick. Teenagers and young adults face sport, sugary drinks, wisdom teeth and orthodontic care, which is where preventive care for young adults focuses. Adults contend with ageing restorations, gum recession, medication-related dry mouth and grinding, which shapes preventive care for adults. Older adults often face dry mouth, root surface decay and the practical challenges of cleaning around bridges and implants.

What Prevention Is Not

It is not a guarantee. People with excellent habits still occasionally need treatment, because genetics, saliva composition and medical circumstances all play a part. Nor is it a reason to defer treatment that is already needed; prevention and treatment run alongside each other.

It is also not something that happens only in a dental chair. The bulk of prevention is daily, at home, and our role is to make sure you know exactly what to do and why.

Making It Practical

A prevention plan only works if it fits your life. Ours usually comes down to a handful of specific actions rather than a long list: the interval for your next visit, one change to your cleaning routine, one change to how often you eat or drink something sweet, and any protective treatment such as a sealant or splint.

We write it down so you are not relying on memory, and we revisit it at each appointment to see what stuck and what did not. If something has not worked, that is information rather than a failing, and we adjust it.

Why Early Intervention Costs Less

The financial argument for prevention is straightforward when you follow one tooth through its possible futures.

Early decay caught at the white spot stage may need nothing more than fluoride and a change in snacking habits. The same decay a year later needs a filling. Left longer, it reaches the dentine and needs a larger filling, then eventually an onlay or a crown. If it reaches the nerve, root canal therapy and a crown follow. If the tooth then fractures, it may be lost, and replacing it is the most expensive outcome of all.

Each step up that ladder costs more, takes longer and is less comfortable than the one before it. Prevention is simply an attempt to stay at the bottom of it.
