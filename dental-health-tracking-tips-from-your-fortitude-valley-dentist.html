How to Keep Track of Your Own Dental Health

31 July 2019·6 min read

Most of what happens to teeth happens slowly. Gums recede a fraction at a time, enamel wears gradually, and decay progresses silently for months. That gradual pace is precisely why changes are easy to miss in the mirror every morning, and why noticing them early is so valuable.

You do not need any equipment or expertise to keep useful track of your own mouth. You need good light, a couple of minutes once a month, and a habit of writing things down.

Do a Monthly Self-Check

Pick a date you will remember, stand in good light, and work through the same sequence each time.

Look at Your Gums

Healthy gums are firm, sit snugly around each tooth and do not bleed when you clean. Check for:

  • Redness or puffiness, particularly along the gum margin
  • Bleeding when you brush or clean between your teeth
  • Gums that appear to be pulling back, making teeth look longer
  • Any lump, blister or pimple-like bump on the gum

Bleeding is the most commonly ignored sign in dentistry. It is not normal, and it is usually the earliest indication that gum and periodontal treatment or better home care is needed.

Look at Your Teeth

  • Chalky white spots or brown marks, especially near the gum line
  • Visible holes, chips or rough edges
  • Teeth that look shorter or flatter than they used to
  • Dark lines at the edges of existing fillings or crowns
  • Any change in colour of a single tooth, which can indicate a nerve problem

Check Your Bite and Jaw

Close your teeth together gently. Does it feel even, or does one tooth meet first? Open wide and note any clicking, catching or limitation. Tenderness in the muscles at the angle of your jaw, especially in the morning, points to grinding and clenching.

Check Your Soft Tissues

Look at your tongue, the inside of your cheeks, the roof of your mouth and under your tongue. You are looking for ulcers that have not healed within two weeks, white or red patches, or any lump. Most findings are harmless, but anything persistent should be checked.

Keep a Simple Record

A note on your phone is enough. Useful things to record:

  • The date you noticed something and where it is
  • What triggers a sensation: cold, heat, sweet, biting, or nothing
  • How long the sensation lasts, in seconds
  • Whether it is getting better, worse or staying the same
  • Any new medication you have started

That last point matters more than most people realise. Dozens of common medications reduce saliva, which significantly raises decay risk and changes what your preventive care for adults should include.

Track Your Habits, Not Just Symptoms

If you want to know why your decay risk is what it is, track your eating occasions for three days rather than what you eat. Note every time something other than water passes your lips, including a biscuit with coffee, a mint, a sip of soft drink.

Most people are surprised. Six to eight separate exposures a day is common, and each one starts an acid attack lasting 20 to 40 minutes. Reducing the number of occasions usually does more for your teeth than changing what you eat.

What Is Worth an Appointment

Book promptly if you notice:

  • Bleeding gums that persist for more than two weeks of good cleaning
  • Sensitivity that lingers after hot or cold
  • Pain on biting or releasing
  • A toothache of any kind, particularly one that wakes you
  • Swelling of the gum or face
  • A tooth that feels loose
  • A chip, crack or lost filling
  • An ulcer that has not healed within two weeks
  • A tooth that has changed colour

Some of these are urgent, some are not, but all are easier to manage early. A small area of decay needs a simple white filling; the same tooth six months later may need a crown or root canal therapy.

What You Cannot Track Yourself

Self-checks have limits, and it is important to be realistic about them. You cannot see between your teeth, where a large share of decay starts. You cannot see beneath fillings and crowns. You cannot measure the bone level around your teeth, or detect an infection at a root tip, or find early decay in the grooves of a back tooth.

That is what a professional check-up and clean is for. Digital X-rays, gum measurements recorded and compared over time, and a systematic examination pick up what no amount of looking in a mirror can.

Use Your Records at Your Appointment

Bring your notes. A dated observation such as "sharp pain on cold, lower right, lasts a few seconds, started three weeks ago" narrows the possibilities immediately and often saves testing time.

It also helps us see patterns. Sensitivity that has worsened steadily is a different story from sensitivity that comes and goes with the weather, and you are the only person who can supply that history.

Set Your Own Reminders

Tracking only works if it happens. Two reminders cover most of it.

Put a recurring monthly reminder in your phone for the self-check, tied to something you already do, such as the first weekend of the month. Two minutes is enough.

Put your next recommended dental visit straight into your calendar when you book it, rather than relying on a reminder arriving at a convenient moment. People who keep consistently to their recommended interval need less treatment over the years, and that is the entire point of monitoring anything.

If you have had a particular concern noted for monitoring, such as an early area of decay or a receding gum margin, ask us what to look for and set a reminder to check it specifically.
