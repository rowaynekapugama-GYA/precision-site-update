3D Printing in Dentistry: Where Things Actually Stand

9 February 2024·7 min read

Dentistry has quietly become a digital profession. Impression putty is disappearing, restorations are designed on screen, and appliances that once took weeks now take days. Three-dimensional printing sits at the centre of a lot of that change, although its role is often misunderstood.

Here is a clear picture of what digital manufacturing is used for in practice today, what it does well, and what it means for you in the chair.

A Brief History of the Crown

Covering a damaged tooth is not a new idea. Gold and other metal tooth coverings date back thousands of years, and for much of that time they were as much a display of wealth as a repair. Dentistry began to take its modern shape in the 18th century, and the 20th century brought porcelain and then high-strength ceramics, which made crowns that look and function like natural teeth possible. Digital design and manufacturing are the latest step in that long progression.

Milling and Printing Are Not the Same Thing

Both are ways of producing a physical object from a digital design, but they work in opposite directions.

Milling is subtractive. A solid block of ceramic is held in a machine and material is cut away until the restoration remains. This is how a CEREC same-day crown is produced, and it is well suited to dense, high-strength ceramics.

Printing is additive. Layers of liquid resin are cured one on top of another until the object is built up. It is excellent for detailed shapes, can produce several items in one run, and wastes very little material.

In most digital practices the two coexist, each used for what it suits.

Where 3D Printing Is Genuinely Useful Today

Models and Guides

Printed models replace the plaster casts once poured from impressions. They are used to check fit, to make appliances over, and to plan complex cases. Printed surgical guides also help position dental implants accurately according to a plan made from 3D imaging.

Clear Aligners

Every set of Invisalign aligners begins with a printed model of the projected position of your teeth at that stage, over which the aligner is formed. The sheer volume of aligner treatment worldwide is one of the main reasons printing scaled up so quickly.

Splints, Retainers and Appliances

Night splints for grinding and clenching, retainers and various orthodontic appliances are increasingly printed directly, with a consistency that hand-made versions cannot always match.

Dentures

Printed denture bases and teeth are now well established. They allow a design to be stored digitally, which means a replacement can be produced without starting from scratch if a denture is lost or broken.

Temporary and Provisional Restorations

Printed temporary crowns and bridges fit well and look good, which matters when someone wears them for weeks while definitive work is made.

Where Printing Is Still Developing

Printed permanent crowns are the area attracting the most attention, and the picture is genuinely promising. Some in-practice systems can now print a hybrid resin crown in under an hour. Resin-ceramic hybrid materials have improved considerably, and regulatory approvals for definitive printed restorations have expanded. Dentistry is, sensibly, a conservative profession, so new materials tend to be adopted gradually as evidence builds.

Even so, the long-term clinical evidence for printed permanent crowns is shorter than it is for milled ceramics and laboratory-made restorations, which have decades of data behind them. Wear resistance, strength under heavy load and colour stability over many years are the questions still being answered.

Our position is straightforward. We use digital technology where the evidence supports it and where it benefits the patient, and we are not in a hurry to be first with something that a patient will rely on for years. For most crowns today, that means a milled ceramic restoration or a laboratory-made one, chosen according to the tooth.

What the Digital Workflow Means for Your Appointment

Whichever manufacturing method follows, the front end of the process has changed for the better.

No Impression Putty

A 3D intra-oral scanner captures your teeth as a digital model in a few minutes. For anyone with a sensitive gag reflex, this is a substantial improvement over a tray of impression material.

Fewer Appointments

When a restoration can be produced in the practice, a crown can often be prepared and fitted in one visit, with no temporary crown and no second appointment.

Accuracy and Repeatability

Digital designs can be checked, refined and reproduced. If a splint is lost, the file exists. If a restoration needs remaking, the design is already there.

Better Conversations

Seeing your teeth rotating on a screen makes it far easier to understand why a particular tooth is a concern, and to compare options for crowns and bridges.

Questions Worth Asking

If you are told a restoration will be printed or milled, reasonable questions include:

  • What material is it, and how long has it been in clinical use?
  • Is this a definitive restoration or a temporary one?
  • What is the expected lifespan, and what happens if it chips?
  • Would a laboratory-made alternative be better for this particular tooth?

The technology used matters less than whether it is the right choice for your situation.

Technology Is a Tool, Not the Treatment

It is easy to be impressed by a machine in the corner of a practice. What determines the outcome, though, is the diagnosis, the preparation of the tooth, the accuracy of the margin and the care taken at the fit. Digital tools support all of that. They do not replace it.

Digital Records Have Their Own Value

One advantage of a digital workflow gets less attention than the machines themselves: the file persists. Once your teeth have been scanned, that record can be reused.

If a night splint is lost on holiday, a replacement can be produced from the stored design rather than starting again. If a denture is broken, the same applies. Where a restoration needs remaking, the original design is a starting point rather than a blank page.

Stored scans also let us compare your teeth over time. Wear from grinding, gum recession and tooth movement all happen slowly enough to be invisible year to year, but obvious when two scans taken several years apart are overlaid. That makes it easier to show you a developing problem early, when the options are still simple.
