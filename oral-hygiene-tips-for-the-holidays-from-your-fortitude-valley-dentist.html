Looking After Your Teeth Over the Holiday Season

10 December 2019·7 min read

The holidays are hard on teeth, and not for the reason most people assume. It is not the volume of sugar so much as the pattern: a picked-at cheese board, a glass topped up over several hours, chocolates within arm's reach all afternoon. Constant small exposures keep the mouth acidic for far longer than one dessert ever would.

None of this means going without. A few adjustments make a genuine difference, and they are all manageable in the middle of a busy season.

Understand the Acid Cycle

Every time you eat or drink something sugary or acidic, the bacteria in plaque produce acid that softens enamel. Saliva then takes around 20 to 40 minutes to neutralise that acid and let the enamel recover.

Eat a dessert in one sitting and your mouth goes through one acid cycle. Graze on the same dessert across three hours and you keep resetting the clock, leaving little recovery time. Frequency matters more than quantity.

Practical version: enjoy the treat, then stop. Give your mouth a break between rounds. Where you can, have sweet things with a meal rather than on their own, as saliva flow is higher while you eat and helps neutralise acid. Balancing indulgence with crunchy fruit and vegetables, such as carrots, also helps clear the teeth.

Drinks Deserve More Attention Than Food

Soft drinks, sparkling wine, cocktails, fruit juice and cordial are all acidic, and sipping them slowly is the equivalent of grazing. Beer, wine and mixers are also drying, which reduces the saliva that would otherwise help.

  • Alternate alcoholic or sweet drinks with plain water
  • Drink water alongside, not just afterwards
  • Use a straw for very acidic drinks where practical
  • Choose sparkling mineral water over soft drink if you like bubbles
  • Remember that sugar-free soft drinks are still acidic and can erode enamel
  • Drink tap water where you can, as fluoridated water helps strengthen enamel
  • Avoid sipping one drink over hours if you can help it

Wait Before Brushing After Acidic Food and Drink

Enamel is temporarily softened after acid exposure, and brushing immediately can wear it. Rinse with water or chew sugar-free gum to get saliva moving, then wait around half an hour before brushing.

Beware the Hard Stuff

Holiday dental emergencies are remarkably predictable. Nut shells, unpopped popcorn kernels, ice, boiled lollies, glacé fruit with pits left in, and the traditional attempt to open a bottle with your teeth account for a good share of them. Sticky toffees and caramels also pull at crowns, bridges, veneers and older fillings, and candy canes are both hard and sticky. Chewing ice can crack enamel, particularly in teeth with large fillings, and the cold can trigger pain in a sensitive tooth. Use scissors for sticky tape and a nutcracker for nuts, and keep your teeth for eating.

If something does break, our Emergency Dentistry team can help, and our page on a broken tooth explains what to do in the meantime.

Keep the Routine Going

Routines are the first casualty of a holiday. Late nights, travel and houseguests all conspire against a two-minute brush.

  • Pack your toothbrush, toothpaste and floss where you will see them, not at the bottom of a bag
  • Keep brushing twice daily and cleaning between your teeth once daily, even when the day has run long
  • Keep a travel toothbrush in the car or handbag if you are away from home
  • If children are staying up later than usual, brush before the day falls apart rather than at bedtime

Small Swaps That Help

Many holiday recipes can be adapted with less sugar or with wholemeal flour, and there are tooth-kinder versions of most shop-bought treats. If you are choosing chocolate, dark chocolate generally contains less sugar than milk or white chocolate. For stockings, a novelty toothbrush, a fun-flavoured floss or a new toothpaste can replace some of the lollies. Red wine and other dark drinks can stain teeth, so a sip of water afterwards helps there too.

Helping Children Through the Season

Children face the same grazing problem, usually with more enthusiasm. A few things help without making you the villain:

  • Set treat times rather than leaving bowls out all day
  • Offer water as the default drink and save juice for mealtimes
  • Serve cheese, nuts and vegetables alongside sweet food, as cheese in particular helps neutralise acid
  • Supervise brushing for younger children, especially when they are overtired
  • Keep up regular children's dentistry visits so anything developing is picked up early

Clenching and the Stress of It All

The holidays are not restful for everyone. Hosting, family logistics and end-of-year deadlines often bring a rise in grinding and clenching, and people frequently arrive in January with jaw ache or a cracked tooth. If you have a night splint, keep wearing it. If you notice morning jaw tightness or headaches, mention it at your next visit.

Plan Around the Practice Calendar

Two practical points worth knowing. First, if you are due for a check-up and clean, booking before the break is sensible, as unused health fund limits generally reset at the end of the benefit year. Second, dental practices, including ours, close over parts of the holiday period, so an ache that has been niggling for weeks is far better dealt with beforehand than on a public holiday. If you cannot fit in a visit before the break, book one for soon after it.

If you are planning teeth whitening before an event, book well ahead, as whitening takes time and is best done when your teeth and gums are healthy.

If Something Goes Wrong

For a broken tooth, a lost filling or a toothache over the break, call us as early as possible and we will do our best to see you. For severe facial swelling affecting breathing or swallowing, uncontrolled bleeding or a suspected jaw fracture, call 000 or go to your nearest hospital emergency department.

Travelling Over the Break

If you are heading away, a few minutes of preparation saves trouble later. Pack a toothbrush, toothpaste and interdental cleaners in your hand luggage rather than checked bags, so a delayed suitcase does not cost you two days of routine. Take your night splint if you wear one, along with its case.

If you are travelling somewhere remote or overseas, it is worth noting where the nearest dental practice is, and checking whether your travel insurance covers emergency dental treatment. Most policies cover pain relief rather than definitive treatment, which is another argument for having a niggling tooth checked before you go.

A small travel kit with dental wax, temporary filling material from a pharmacy and simple pain relief covers most short-term problems until you can be seen.
