Sugar and Your Teeth: What Actually Happens

10 June 2019·6 min read

"Sugar causes cavities" is true but unhelpfully vague. Understanding the mechanism changes what you do about it, because it turns out that when and how often you eat sugar matters more than how much.

The Process, Step by Step

Your teeth are covered by a thin film of bacteria called plaque. Certain bacteria within it, particularly Streptococcus mutans, metabolise sugars and produce acid as a by-product.

That acid lowers the pH at the tooth surface. Below roughly pH 5.5, minerals begin to dissolve out of the enamel. This is demineralisation.

Saliva then does the repair work. It neutralises the acid and carries calcium, phosphate and fluoride back into the enamel surface, which is remineralisation. The process takes somewhere between 20 and 40 minutes after the sugar has gone.

A cavity forms when demineralisation consistently outpaces remineralisation over weeks and months.

Why Frequency Beats Quantity

This is the practical consequence. Each separate exposure starts a new acid cycle, regardless of how small it was.

Eating a whole dessert in one sitting produces one cycle. Eating the same amount as six separate nibbles across an afternoon produces six, with little recovery time between them. The second pattern is considerably more damaging despite identical total sugar.

The same applies to drinks. A soft drink finished with a meal is one exposure. The same drink sipped over two hours keeps the mouth acidic almost continuously.

Which Sugars Matter

For teeth, the distinction that matters is not natural versus refined. Bacteria metabolise sucrose, glucose, fructose and the sugars released from cooked starches in much the same way.

What does change the risk is physical form and context:

  • Sticky and retentive foods, such as dried fruit, muesli bars and toffee, hold sugar against the teeth for longer
  • Liquids coat everything, including the surfaces between teeth
  • Sugars eaten with a meal are less damaging than the same sugars eaten alone, because increased saliva flow at mealtimes helps buffer and clear them
  • Acidic sugary drinks do double damage, eroding enamel directly as well as feeding bacteria

Fresh fruit contains sugar but also water and fibre, stimulates chewing and saliva, and is far less problematic than its dried equivalent.

Sugar and Gums

Sugar's main effect is on decay rather than directly on gums, but there is an indirect link. A diet high in sugar supports plaque accumulation, and plaque drives the inflammation that leads to gum disease. There is also evidence that high-sugar diets contribute to systemic inflammation, which may affect the body's response to periodontal infection. Daily cleaning and gum and periodontal treatment where needed remain the primary defence.

Practical Changes That Work

You do not need to eliminate sugar, and advice framed that way tends to be ignored.

  • Reduce the number of occasions. Keep sweet things to mealtimes rather than spread across the day
  • Make water the default drink. Between meals especially
  • Finish, do not graze. A treat eaten in one go is better than the same treat rationed over hours
  • Rinse with water afterwards, and wait 30 minutes before brushing if the food or drink was acidic
  • Chew sugar-free gum after meals to stimulate saliva when brushing is not possible
  • Check labels on weekly regulars, since cereal, yoghurt, sauces and bread account for more than people expect
  • Choose fresh fruit over dried

A Three-Day Exercise Worth Doing

If you want to know your own risk, do not track what you eat. Track how often.

For three days, note every occasion something other than plain water enters your mouth: the biscuit with coffee, the mint, the sip of juice, the handful of nuts, the glass of wine. Write down the times.

Most people are surprised to find six to eight separate occasions a day, sometimes more. Each one starts an acid cycle lasting 20 to 40 minutes, so the mouth may be acidic for a substantial part of the day.

Reducing the number of occasions, even without changing what you eat, often does more for your teeth than any product you can buy.

Who Needs to Be More Careful

Risk is not equal. People with reduced saliva from medication, those with many existing restorations, people with gum recession exposing root surfaces, and anyone with recent decay all have less margin for error. Your preventive care for adults plan should reflect that, and children's enamel is more vulnerable again, which is why children's dentistry places so much emphasis on snacking patterns.

If Decay Has Already Started

Early white spot lesions can sometimes be arrested with fluoride and reduced snacking frequency. Once a cavity has formed, it needs restoring, usually with a white filling. Regular check-up and clean visits are what catch it at the stage where the repair is small.

Sugar Substitutes

A common follow-up question is whether sweeteners are a safe alternative for teeth, and the short answer is that they are, with one caveat.

Non-sugar sweeteners are not metabolised by oral bacteria into acid, so they do not drive decay. Xylitol has been studied for a possible protective effect, and sugar-free gum containing it stimulates saliva, which helps.

The caveat is acidity. Many sugar-free drinks are just as acidic as their sugared equivalents, and acid erodes enamel without any bacterial involvement. A sugar-free soft drink sipped across an afternoon still does damage, just by a different route.
