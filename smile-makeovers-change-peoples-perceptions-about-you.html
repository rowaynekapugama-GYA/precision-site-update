Smiles, Confidence and What Research Actually Shows

3 July 2019·6 min read

People considering cosmetic treatment often describe the same thing: not wanting a different face, but wanting to stop thinking about their teeth. Covering the mouth when laughing, angling away in photographs, keeping the lips closed in a work headshot.

It is worth looking honestly at what the evidence says about smiles and perception, and at what dental treatment can and cannot contribute.

What the Research Suggests

Studies in social psychology have found that facial appearance, including the mouth and teeth, influences first impressions, and that these impressions form very quickly. Research has also found associations between dental appearance and judgements people make about traits such as friendliness, health and social confidence.

Those findings are real, but they should be read carefully. They describe first impressions in controlled conditions, often using photographs, and first impressions are only a small part of how people come to know each other. Nobody should expect a change in dental appearance to reshape their life.

The more consistent finding, and the one that matters most in practice, concerns the person themselves. People who are self-conscious about their teeth frequently modify their behaviour to hide them, and that self-consciousness tends to ease when the thing they were conscious of is addressed.

What Cosmetic Treatment Can Realistically Change

  • Colour, through professional teeth whitening or, where whitening is insufficient, veneers
  • Shape and proportion, through composite resin veneers, porcelain veneers or conservative reshaping
  • Alignment, through Invisalign or other orthodontic treatment
  • Repairing damage, where chips, wear or old restorations stand out
  • Gum appearance, where health or contour is affecting how teeth look

Our cosmetic dentistry page explains each option in more detail.

What It Cannot Change

Dental treatment does not fix how you feel about yourself generally, and it should not be sold as though it does. Where someone's dissatisfaction is focused on a specific, identifiable feature, treatment often helps. Where it is diffuse, or where expectations are unrealistic, treatment rarely resolves it, and sometimes the focus simply moves elsewhere.

That is not a reason to avoid cosmetic dentistry. It is a reason to be clear about what you want to change and why, and for a practitioner to listen properly rather than agreeing with everything.

How We Approach a Cosmetic Consultation

We Ask What Bothers You

Specific answers are the most useful ones: the colour of one tooth, a chip that catches the light, the way the front teeth overlap. Vague dissatisfaction is harder to treat and worth exploring before any treatment is planned.

Health Comes First

Every cosmetic plan starts with a full examination. Decay, gum inflammation and grinding all need addressing first, both for health reasons and because cosmetic work lasts longer in a stable mouth. A check-up and clean alone sometimes improves appearance more than people expect, and where the gums are the issue, gum and periodontal treatment comes before anything else.

We Explain the Trade-Offs

Every option costs something, in money and often in tooth structure. We will tell you when a simpler treatment would achieve most of what you want, and we will tell you when what you are asking for would require removing healthy enamel we would rather preserve.

No Pressure

You will receive a written plan and the time to think about it. A cosmetic decision should still make sense to you a month later. If it does not, that is useful information.

Where Function and Appearance Overlap

It is worth noting that many treatments people think of as cosmetic also do structural work.

Rebuilding worn front edges restores tooth length and protects what remains. Straightening crowded teeth makes them easier to clean, which reduces decay and gum problems. Replacing an old, failing restoration improves both appearance and the seal protecting the tooth.

This overlap often changes the conversation. A treatment that improves appearance and also addresses a genuine clinical issue is usually an easier decision than one that is purely elective.

A Note on Before and After Images

Under Australian advertising rules for regulated health services, before and after images must be genuine, unedited, taken under consistent conditions, carry appropriate consent, and be accompanied by a statement that results vary between individuals. Images that have been retouched or taken under flattering lighting are not permitted, and testimonials about clinical treatment cannot be used in advertising at all.

It is worth keeping that in mind when assessing any cosmetic marketing, including ours.

Questions to Ask Yourself First

Before committing to cosmetic treatment, a few questions tend to clarify matters.

What specifically would you change, in one sentence? If you cannot name it, spend more time thinking before spending money.

Is this something you have wanted for a long time, or a recent reaction to a photograph or comment? Both are legitimate, but the first tends to produce more satisfaction.

What would you still be unhappy about afterwards? An honest answer here is the most useful predictor of how you will feel at the end.

What are you willing to maintain? Every cosmetic result requires upkeep, and some options require more than others.
