Professional Whitening Compared With the Alternatives

29 April 2019·6 min read

Whitening is the most requested cosmetic treatment in dentistry, and the options range from a supermarket strip to a supervised course of treatment. They are not equivalent, and the differences are worth understanding before you spend money on either.

Why Teeth Darken

Two different processes are involved, and they respond differently to whitening.

Extrinsic staining sits on the surface, picked up from coffee, tea, red wine, dark sauces and tobacco. Much of this responds to a professional clean and polish.

Intrinsic discolouration comes from within the tooth. Enamel thins with age, allowing the naturally yellower dentine beneath to show through. Trauma can darken a single tooth, and certain medications taken during tooth development can cause banding or greyness. Intrinsic colour is what whitening gel actually targets, and it responds to different degrees depending on the cause.

How Whitening Works

Whitening gels release oxygen molecules that break down the pigmented compounds inside the tooth structure. The concentration of the gel and the length of contact determine the effect, which is why a well-fitting tray matters as much as the product.

The Options Compared

Professional Take-Home Whitening

Custom trays are made from a scan or impression of your teeth, so the gel stays in contact with the tooth surface and away from your gums. You wear them for a set time each day over one to two weeks.

Professional take-home whitening is the option we recommend most often. It is gradual, controllable, and the trays can be reused later for occasional top-ups.

In-Chair Whitening

Gel is applied in the practice with your gums and lips carefully isolated, under supervision. It suits people who want to start with a noticeable change quickly, and it is often combined with take-home trays to maintain the result.

Supermarket and Pharmacy Kits

These use lower concentrations and, more importantly, a one-size tray or strip that does not match your teeth. Gel escapes onto the gums, causing irritation, and does not sit evenly on the tooth surfaces. Results are usually modest and patchy. They also come with no examination, so an underlying problem can be missed entirely.

Non-Dental Salon Treatments

In Australia, only registered dental practitioners may use higher-concentration whitening products. Services offered outside a dental setting are limited in what they can legally apply, and no clinical examination is involved.

Charcoal Pastes and DIY Remedies

Charcoal and bicarbonate pastes remove some surface stain by abrasion, but abrasion also wears enamel, and thinner enamel looks more yellow, not less. Lemon juice, vinegar and similar remedies erode enamel directly. None of these change the internal colour of the tooth.

Why the Examination Comes First

This is the part that gets skipped with over-the-counter products, and it matters.

Whitening gel reaching an untreated cavity, an exposed root surface or an inflamed gum causes real discomfort. Decay needs treating first, and if your gums are inflamed, gum and periodontal treatment or a professional clean comes before whitening.

Whitening also has no effect on existing restorations. Fillings, crowns and veneers stay exactly the same shade, so if you have white fillings or veneers on your front teeth, they may look darker afterwards and need replacing to match. We would rather tell you that beforehand than have you discover it.

Some discolouration responds poorly to whitening, particularly certain internal stains, and in those cases composite resin veneers or porcelain veneers may be a more realistic route. Again, better to know first.

What to Expect

Results vary from person to person depending on the type of staining, your natural tooth colour and how consistently you follow the plan. Yellowish teeth generally respond better than grey ones.

Temporary sensitivity is the most common side effect, and a desensitising toothpaste usually manages it. We record your starting shade so you can see the change objectively rather than relying on memory.

Whitening is not permanent. Teeth gradually pick up stain again, and the rate depends on your diet and habits. Occasional top-ups with your trays maintain the result, and regular check-ups and cleans help.

Who Should Not Whiten

Whitening is generally not recommended during pregnancy or breastfeeding, for children, or for anyone with untreated decay or gum disease. If you have significant sensitivity, tell us, as the approach can often be adjusted.

Maintaining Your Result

Whitening is not a one-off purchase so much as a starting point with upkeep. Once you reach a shade you are happy with, a short top-up with your custom trays every several months usually holds it, and because you already have the trays, the ongoing cost is only the gel.

Habits make a large difference to how quickly the shade fades. Coffee, tea, red wine, dark cola and smoking are the usual culprits. You do not need to avoid them, but rinsing with water afterwards helps, and drinking through a straw reduces contact with the front teeth.

Regular professional cleans also matter, because surface stain accumulates steadily and polishing removes it. Many people find their teeth look noticeably brighter after a clean alone, before any whitening gel is involved.
